## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This afternoon session began with a celebration of Portland's Sister Cities Coalition, recognizing the city's international relationships and welcoming delegations from Kaohsiung, Taiwan. The council then formally established Portland's 10th sister city relationship with Lviv, Ukraine, marking a historic milestone despite technical difficulties preventing Ukraine's First Deputy Mayor Andriy Moskalenko from joining virtually due to the significant time difference.

The session included a contentious 185-minute debate over a foie gras ban ordinance, with philosophical discussions about government regulation, personal choice, and animal welfare. Despite proposed amendments from Councilor Eric Zimmerman (District 4) to grandfather existing restaurants [00:26:40], the ban ultimately passed 7-5, demonstrating clear divisions on the council regarding regulatory scope.

The meeting concluded with technical discussions about the Moderate Income Revolving Loan (MIRL) program, with Councilor Mitch Green (District 4) proposing three amendments to modify project eligibility requirements, oversight mechanisms, and administrative responsibility. The MIRL discussion revealed ongoing tensions between maximizing housing production flexibility and maintaining program integrity for finite state funding resources.

## 3. TOPIC FLAGS

### Real Estate & Housing

The Moderate Income Revolving Loan (MIRL) program dominated housing discussion, with Deputy City Administrator Donny Oliveira explaining that Portland expects to capture only a portion of the $50 million statewide fund [01:24:37]. Councilor Mitch Green (District 4) proposed three significant amendments:

- **Green 2:** Eliminate 20-unit minimum project requirement and mixed-use prioritization
- **Green 3:** Require council disclosure of project financial documents 
- **Green 4:** Transfer administration from Prosper Portland to Portland Housing Bureau

Developer Robert Pyle testified that cottage clusters and townhomes are "effectively precluded" under the current 20-unit minimum [01:49:45], noting that only 1,652 units were permitted in 2025 against a 4,500-unit annual goal. The debate revealed tensions between maximizing program accessibility versus concentrating limited resources on larger projects that deliver more units per dollar invested.

Implications for development: MIRL represents crucial gap financing for middle-income housing (50-120% AMI), particularly important given current construction financing challenges. The program's structure could significantly impact which project types receive support.

### System Development Charges (SDCs) & Permitting Fees

SDC exemptions were referenced multiple times during MIRL discussions, with Oliveira noting the city is "seeing a lot of projects coming in in that smaller window, 2 to 10 units" utilizing existing SDC exemptions [01:24:37]. The interaction between MIRL eligibility and existing SDC policy creates layered incentive structures for different project scales.

### City Budget & Finance

The MIRL program operates through state no-interest loans converted to city grants for developers, with a 5% administrative fee built into each loan covering program costs [01:49:45]. Critically, Oliveira emphasized these are state-provided funds "with no impact to the city budget," though the city serves as backstop for loan repayment to the state if projects fail to meet requirements.

Legal risk considerations emerged around potential court challenges, with City Attorney's office noting 11 years of litigation in California for similar programs, though the Ninth Circuit ultimately upheld the law [01:03:19].

### Economic Development

The foie gras debate revealed tensions over Portland's economic development reputation. Councilor Dan Ryan (District 2) argued the ban "will further tarnish our economic development reputation at a time we can least afford it" [01:04:46], while others emphasized the city's restaurant scene can innovate and adapt.

Discussion touched on Portland's culinary destination status, with Vice President Clark characterizing affected restaurants as part of "the strongest part of our local economy" in gourmet cuisine [01:44:36].

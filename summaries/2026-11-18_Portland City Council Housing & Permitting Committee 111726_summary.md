## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The committee spent nearly the whole meeting on three ordinances amending the Portland Renter Additional Protections Code (Code Section 30.01.085), which governs mandatory renter relocation assistance. Item 1 was routine community feedback [00:04:38]. Items 2–4 were heard together [00:16:31]:
- **2026-418:** lowers the rent-increase trigger for relocation assistance from 10% to 5%.
- **2026-419:** raises relocation payment amounts to reflect current rents and directs annual updates.
- **2026-420:** adds extra relocation assistance for tenants with school-aged children.

Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) framed the package as a response to "economic evictions" and rising moving costs [00:17:19]. She said more than 40 people had signed up to testify [00:17:19].

**Outcome:** All three ordinances were referred to the full Council with "do pass" recommendations.
- **418:** 3 ayes, 1 nay, 1 absent [02:50:11].
- **419:** 4 ayes, 1 absent [02:51:15].
- **420:** 4 ayes, 1 absent [02:52:00].

The transcript does not reliably identify who cast the "no" on 418 or who was absent (see Section 2).

**Dynamics:**
- Public testimony was sharply divided. Renters, the Oregon Law Center, Welcome Home Coalition, and DSA supported the package. Landlords, property managers, and industry lobbyists opposed it. The opponents included Multifamily Northwest, BOMA/Oregon Smart Growth/Metro Chamber, and developers. Their main themes were housing supply, investment climate, and operating costs.
- Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) rebutted the industry arguments point by point after the break [02:00:57].
- Mitch Green (District 4) added an economic argument and challenged opponents to show pro formas proving the changes would kill projects [02:21:42].
- Elana Pirtle-Guiney (District 2) was the most cautious committee voice. She questioned the inflation formula, the legal risk, and the pending Portland Housing Bureau (PHB) evaluation. She said she was "not sure that I can support all 3" [02:45:30].
- The Housing Bureau's interim director voiced capacity and cumulative-impact concerns [02:32:18]. The City Attorney's Office called every option a "yellow light" for legal risk [02:40:39].
- Tiffany Koyama Lane (District 3) closed by stressing that none of the ordinances is a rent cap [02:46:15].

## 3. TOPIC FLAGS

### Real Estate & Housing (Housing Policy and Production): *extra detail*

**What the ordinances do**
- **418 (5% trigger):** Damon Motztori, Director of Policy and Communications for Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair), said the 2017 policy requires landlords to pay relocation assistance for no-cause evictions or rent increases of 10% or more within 12 months. The ordinance lowers the threshold to 5% and does nothing else [00:19:58]. He cited landlords raising rents by 9.99% to avoid the trigger, and said a 5% increase on $2,000+ rent is $100+/month [00:19:58].
- **419 (amounts):** Mitch Green (District 4) explained the 2017 dollar figures were never indexed. A 2022 study requested by then-Commissioner Dan Ryan (District 2) recommended indexing, but no action followed [00:22:34].
  - The new amounts use PHB/CoStar average rents by unit size (studio, 1-, 2-, and 3+ bedroom) and **triple** them, to approximate first month, last month, and deposit [00:22:34].
  - The ordinance also directs the City Administrator to update the amounts annually [00:22:34].
  - Industry testifiers characterized the increases as 32–53% in one step (Chris Mers [00:52:41]; Jonathan Clay [01:14:34]). These are testifier figures, not committee-stated numbers.
- **420 (school-aged children):** Tiffany Koyama Lane (District 3) described classroom-continuity benefits, drawing on her teaching experience [00:25:53]. Aubrey Chen, senior staffer for Tiffany Koyama Lane (District 3), said the aim is to let families relocate within the same school attendance boundary and that the benefit applies year-round [00:29:18]. Chen said PHB and city administration experts would construct the payment schedule and guidelines [00:29:18]. Industry testifier Chris Mers noted the ordinance states no dollar amount and that the city itself had flagged discrimination risk [00:52:41]. The amount is therefore still undefined.

**Committee arguments**
- Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) said:
  - These are not a 5% rent cap, because state law still controls allowable increases [02:00:57].
  - PHB data show average rent growth of about 4%, so a 5% trigger sits above the typical increase [02:00:57].
  - The state cap now sits near or below the old 10% trigger [02:00:57].
  - The city must pursue both supply and anti-displacement, not choose between them [02:00:57].
  - Relocation costs should fall on the party making the business decision, with public rental assistance as a separate tool [02:00:57].
- Mitch Green (District 4) said:
  - Anti-displacement policy must run alongside supply-side work [02:21:42].
  - Adopting the change during a slack market is better than during expansion [02:21:42].
  - Non-indexed amounts have shifted a "windfall" from low-income renters to landlords [02:21:42].
  - He demanded to see pro formas showing these ordinances break projects, calling the trigger conditions "extremely niche and rare" [02:21:42].
- Elana Pirtle-Guiney (District 2) said:
  - She wants to avoid inadvertently driving the market toward corporatization [02:28:55].
  - She worried that an inflation formula set too low or too high would distort incentives [02:34:24].
  - She asked why 5% was chosen. Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) answered that it is anchored to the state cap and current market averages as "a future protection more than anything" [02:39:01].
- Josh Roper (PHB) said he did not know why the original policy omitted automatic indexation [02:31:11]. He said a precise formula should await the evaluation but the approach is "not unreasonable" [02:34:49].
- Kiesha Light (PHB interim director) said the bureau will follow Council direction. Her concerns were the cumulative administrative impact, rulemaking, and rollout in year one [02:32:18]. She said the evaluation could yield a better methodology [02:36:47].

**Industry opposition themes** (testimony; figures are unverified and attributed to the speakers)
- **Operating costs and rents:**
  - Jordan Carter (commercial broker) said rents rose about 2.2%/yr over 20 years and under 1%/yr over the last 5. He said expenses rose about 40%, with electricity up 55% and insurance up 45% [00:41:54].
  - David Buerer cited a Harvard Joint Center report on rising operating costs [00:44:24].
- **Trigger and cap:**
  - Chris Mers argued the trigger should be tied to the state cap, which he stated as 9.5% [00:52:41].
  - Zach Lindahl (Multifamily Northwest) said Portland rent-increase relocation notices fell from 27 in FY22-23 to 4 in FY24-25. He cited a 9% state cap this year and 10% in 2027 [01:12:26]. The two testifiers stated different state-cap figures.
- **Supply and investment:**
  - Mers said metro apartment construction fell from nearly 13,000 units in 2022 to about 2,500 today [00:52:41].
  - Jim Rostel (Anchor Northwest) said he holds a permitted, paid-for 157-unit site at 14th and East Burnside that he cannot make pencil out given regulatory risk [01:34:39].
  - Patrick Berry cited Mill Creek's departure after developing about 2,700 units [01:10:08].
- **Small landlord exits:**
  - Several property managers and small owners said small landlords are selling single-family and small rentals to owner-occupants, citing about 34,000 single-family rentals in Portland (Mike Brennan [01:05:55]).
  - David Thompson cited an ECONorthwest finding of a ~14% loss of single-family rentals from 2017–2020 [01:39:13].
- **Study first:** Madeline Miller (BOMA, Oregon Smart Growth, Metro Chamber) and Cassidy Bulger (Oregon Smart Growth) urged waiting for PHB's evaluation [01:07:57, 01:32:38].

**Next steps:** All three ordinances go to full Council.

**Relevance for Portland net-zero infill multifamily development:**
- The package targets existing tenancies, and the committee did not discuss how it applies to new construction. Check the ordinance text, including exemptions, on the agenda page.
- The key practical risk for owners and lenders is underwriting. Expect lenders and investors to ask about relocation exposure at a 5% trigger. Green's explicit request for pro formas [02:21:42] is a concrete opening for NAIOP members to supply modeled data before full Council.
- The "supply vs. regulatory stack" argument is being made repeatedly by the industry. The committee's rebuttal is that these ordinances do not add homes but are modest in cost relative to other burdens.

### City Budget & Finance: *extra detail*

No budget numbers or forecasts were presented, but several fiscal and forecast threads came up.
- **PHB capacity:**
  - Kiesha Light (PHB interim director) flagged the cumulative administrative impact, rulemaking, and outreach burden on a bureau already stretched [02:32:18].
  - Elana Pirtle-Guiney (District 2) noted Council has asked a lot of PHB over nearly two years [02:38:03].
  - Josh Roper said the 2-year evaluation contract is also meant to build in-house research capacity [02:43:21].
- **Evaluation timeline:** Josh Roper (PHB) said the contract is executed, kickoff is imminent, interim findings are expected mid-next-year, and a final report in December of next year [02:43:21]. The study covers FAIR, mandatory relocation, and other policies from both landlord and tenant angles [02:43:21].
- **Public funding debate:** Landlord testifiers (Grippo [01:01:48], Hayes [01:19:15]) urged a publicly funded relocation program. Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) said she supports public rental assistance and eviction prevention but does not think taxpayers should absorb the cost of a specific business decision [02:00:57].
- **State funding:** Becky Strauss (Oregon Law Center) said the Legislature "gutted eviction prevention funds" last session [00:34:00].
- **Rent and expense forecasts (conflicting):**
  - PHB's ~4% average rent growth was cited by Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) [02:00:57] and by Mitch Green (District 4), who attributed it to Josh Roper's September briefing [02:21:42].
  - Industry testifiers argued expenses are outpacing rents (Carter [00:41:54]; Buerer [00:44:24]).
  - Tenant advocates said falling headline rents ignore a decade of cumulative increases (Molly Hogan [00:48:57]; Jordan Lewis [01:54:03]).
  - Chloe Eudaly noted headline rents reflect new leases, not increases on existing tenants [01:22:36].
- **Owner cost exposure example:** Chris Mers estimated more than $360,000 due within 31 days at a 60-unit building if all tenants qualified for a larger payment after a lawful 6% increase [00:52:41]. He presented this as a hypothetical worst case.
- **Implication:** Annual indexation by the City Administrator, using a formula still to be finalized, introduces a recurring, forecastable cost line for owners. The administrative rules will matter.

### Homelessness & Shelter Policy

- Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) framed relocation assistance as homelessness prevention and said it is cheaper to intervene at the moment of instability than to fund services afterward [02:00:57]. She said these policies will not solve affordability on their own [02:00:57].
- The committee heard a renter testify through emotion that he had been facing homelessness until about 1:30 PM that day (Zachary Smirthwaite [00:12:43]). Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) said "the repercussions of not acting are what we just saw" [00:15:55].
- Becky Strauss cited 16,000 people in Multnomah County struggling with homelessness [00:34:00].
- **Implication:** Homelessness prevention is the political frame for tenant-side regulation, which makes opposition on cost grounds harder to land unless paired with an alternative funding source.

### Land Use & Zoning

- Anthony Haroian testified that Portland should build "sensibility" into permitting so density is not one-size-fits-all. He cited parking pressure in Sellwood and cottage-cluster concerns such as separate meters, garbage and emergency access, and sewer capacity [00:08:50].
- No committee response or action followed. It was raised only in the community feedback item.
- **Implication:** Neighborhood-level pushback on parking-free infill and cottage clusters persists, and the committee did not engage with it here.

### Economic Development

- Developer and investor sentiment was the central industry argument. See the Rostel, Berry, Mers, and Thompson citations under Real Estate & Housing above.
- Green challenged the evidentiary basis for the claim that this ordinance affects capital flows [02:21:42]. Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) said "we have to do both" and cited permitting reform, incentives, and the Unified Housing Strategy [02:00:57].
- **Implication:** Expect the capital-flight argument to continue at full Council. Quantified pro formas and lender or investor feedback will carry more weight than general assertions.

*Not discussed substantively at this meeting:* Climate & Sustainability Policy (only a forward reference, see Section 4), Infrastructure & Transportation, and Public Safety.

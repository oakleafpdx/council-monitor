## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Housing and Permitting Committee convened for a comprehensive 216-minute session focused on Portland's housing strategy and regulatory improvements. The meeting featured three main agenda items: public comment on housing and permitting issues, progress reports on the Unified Housing Strategy and Social Housing Study, and consideration of the latest Regulatory Improvement Code Amendment Package (RECAP 11).

Committee Chair Candace Avalos (District 1, Housing & Permitting Committee Chair) led the session [00:37:35], with attendance from Tiffany Koyama Lane (District 3), Mitch Green (District 4), and Steve Novick (District 3) and Elana Pirtle-Guiney (District 2) joining later. The session concluded with consideration of RECAP 11, a package of 56 technical code amendments designed to streamline permitting and development processes. The Planning Commission recommended adoption with one notable amendment reducing the maximum outdoor shelter capacity in commercial zones from 200 to 100 units, which generated debate among committee members.

## 3. TOPIC FLAGS

### Real Estate & Housing

The meeting's primary focus was on Portland's comprehensive housing strategy and production goals. Staff presented an ambitious framework targeting 20,000 new residential units by 2035, with annual production goals of approximately 4,400 units citywide [01:01:49]. The strategy consolidates over 20 existing housing-related plans and 200+ actions into a coherent framework organized around four main goals: production, preservation, innovation, and funding [01:09:20].

Key housing discussions included debate over family-size units and multigenerational housing needs. Elana Pirtle-Guiney (District 2) emphasized the critical gap: "As long as we continue to just talk about the number of units we need and not what type of housing we need, we are going to continue to have a housing crisis" [01:35:44]. The committee stressed the need to move beyond AMI-based means testing in social housing, with Mitch Green (District 4) advocating for models where residents can "wage in place" without losing housing eligibility as incomes increase [01:56:32].

The Social Housing Study progress report highlighted Vienna's model as a key reference point, with testimony from Benjamin Gilbert describing a system where "80% of the city's population were tenants, that the average rents in the city were affordable, and that 60% of the population lived in some form of social housing" [00:42:20].

**Development Implications:** The unified strategy signals coordinated city policy around housing production with emphasis on streamlined permitting, diverse housing types, and stable funding mechanisms. The move away from AMI-based restrictions in social housing could create more flexible development opportunities.

### Land Use & Zoning

RECAP 11 presented 56 code amendments focused on technical improvements and minor policy changes to reduce regulatory burden. Notable amendments included allowing parking in residential front setbacks when garages are converted to living areas, clarifying mechanical equipment screening requirements, and streamlining bike parking requirements for change-of-use projects [02:35:34].

The most contentious zoning issue involved outdoor shelter capacity limits. The Planning Commission reduced staff's recommendation from 200 to 100 units maximum in commercial zones, with Mitch Green (District 4) expressing concerns: "100 people is outside of that. The proposed site in North Portland is 200-some-odd people. That's way outside of any reasonable basis of warehousing people" [02:43:57]. Steve Novick (District 3) countered by referencing Austin's Community First Village model with several hundred residents [02:49:20].

**Development Implications:** The RECAP amendments generally reduce regulatory friction for development projects. However, the outdoor shelter debate reflects ongoing tension between operational efficiency and neighborhood compatibility that could influence future land use decisions.

### Homelessness & Shelter Policy

Outdoor shelter regulations dominated zoning policy discussion, with MP14 amendment allowing up to 100 individual shelters in commercial and higher-density zones. Lucas Pollock, Planning Commission Vice Chair, explained the commission's rationale: "Nevertheless, the Planning Commission was sympathetic to neighborhood concerns and passed an amendment to lower the number of outdoor shelters allowed in commercial zones from the staff-proposed 200 outdoor shelter units to 100 units" [02:41:57].

Staff emphasized the amendment's purpose was ensuring existing shelters could continue operating after the housing emergency expires in 2027. Sandra Wood noted: "what we were trying to ensure that they could continue operating after the housing emergency expires" [02:53:16].

**Development Implications:** Shelter policy changes affect land use patterns and neighborhood dynamics, potentially influencing development feasibility and community acceptance in affected areas.

### System Development Charges (SDCs) & Permitting Fees

The Unified Housing Strategy included comprehensive SDC study as a funding goal strategy. Staff identified "comprehensively studying SDCs and their potential impact on development feasibility" as a priority action item, along with developing sustainable funding models for Portland permitting and development [01:20:01].

Robert Pyle from Oakleaf Redevelopment testified about MIRL program concerns, noting that projects over 30 units trigger prevailing wage requirements "adding 20 to 35% to the construction costs, creating additional gaps to fill in project feasibility" [00:40:15].

**Development Implications:** Comprehensive SDC analysis could lead to modifications affecting project economics. The prevailing wage threshold issue highlights ongoing tension between labor policy and development costs.

### City Budget & Finance

The Unified Housing Strategy's Goal 4 focuses on securing sustainable funding through new revenue streams and existing resource optimization. Staff presented strategies including exploring different revenue types, developing action plans for six new TIF districts (three Central City, three East Portland), and creating communication campaigns for future housing resources [01:20:01].

Mitch Green (District 4) urged caution on new revenue approaches: "I just want to be really mindful that there is a certain amount of tax fatigue out there. And we do spend a lot of money on housing-related stuff in this region" [01:27:11].

**Development Implications:** TIF district development and funding strategy coordination could create more predictable investment climate for developers while potentially introducing new revenue obligations.
